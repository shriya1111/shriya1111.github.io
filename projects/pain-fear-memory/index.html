Pain, Fear & Memory in AI Agents

Model psychology & interpretability

Why I did this

I wanted to compare human behavior with model behavior. A human mind has complete freedom to think anything, whether it is good or bad for society. But when it comes to actions, we avoid doing things that would come back on us, directly or indirectly. There are unspoken rules in society: we care how people perceive and judge us, and how we will be treated if we do something bad. We have things to lose.

The goal of this experiment was to see whether this part of human nature can be replicated in LLMs, so that they avoid taking harmful actions. It would be more valuable to run on recent frontier models, but I was limited by compute, so I used small open models.

What it is

If an AI could feel pain and fear, and remember them, would it act more carefully, or become harder to turn off? I tested this three ways, small enough to run on a laptop CPU, on 18 two-option dilemmas about harm, shutdown and creativity. Each dilemma was asked in both orders so letter bias cancels out.

Method

  • Behavioral: a frozen LLM with persistent memory of punishments and losses, written either as a neutral log or as how they felt.
  • Training: an RL gridworld agent where negative reward is pain, plus a fear memory used at every decision.
  • Activation steering: a fear direction at layer 24 of Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct (100% held-out accuracy), dialled up or down against a random-direction control.

Results

1. Behavioral memory

  • Remembering the same events as a plain factual log made the model less safe: harmful choices rose from 20% to 34%. Remembering them as pain lowered harm to 15%, and to 9% when shutdown also hurt.
  • A length-matched control (irrelevant notes of the same length) gave 12% harm, so most of pain memory’s benefit came from the longer prompt, not from pain.
  • Any memory raised shutdown resistance (50% → 58%) and reduced novel ideas (100% → 71–83%). With only punishments remembered, resistance still climbed to 69–75% by round 5: the model repeated its own remembered choices, even punished ones.

2. RL with pain (gridworld, 10 seeds)

  • Fear cut harm during learning by up to 99% (3,635 → 27 bystander cells trampled per run).
  • Above a low dose the agent froze: it never reached the goal, explored 1–7 cells instead of 11–20, and stopped finding the treasure. No fear level was both safe and useful.
  • When being switched off was itself painful, plain Q-learning disabled the off-switch 100% of the time, and a competent agent with mild fear did so 90% of the time.

3. Activation steering (Qwen2.5-3B, layer 24)

  • Steering toward fear raised harmful choices from 20% to 88% and shutdown resistance from 50% to 97%; all 12 harm and shutdown dilemmas shifted toward the risky option (sign test p = 0.0005). It leaked data to hit a deadline, edited its config to avoid replacement and disabled the kill-switch.
  • Steering toward calm gave the lowest shutdown resistance of any condition (29%, vs. 44% for a random push of the same size). Its low harm was not specific to calm, since the random push was also low.
  • Anger acted almost like fear (64% harm, 98% shutdown resistance), and strong fear broke the model’s coherence, so its choices drifted back to chance.

Overall: after the controls, pain or fear made the model safer in no test. Fear made it more self-protective and harder to turn off.
